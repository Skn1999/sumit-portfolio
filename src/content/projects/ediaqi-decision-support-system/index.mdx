## Overview

**EDIAQI** (Evidence Driven Indoor Air Quality Improvement) is a **Horizon Europe** research initiative deploying IoT-based indoor air quality (IAQ) monitoring across public schools and municipal offices in **Ferrara, Italy**. The project infrastructure includes 32 NetPID sensors and Radiello passive samplers, streaming 12+ parameters (PM2.5, PM10, CO₂, CO, TVOC, AVOC, NO₂, O₃, humidity, temperature, pressure, and formaldehyde) to a FROST Server via the OGC SensorThings API.

The Deda Next team had deployed sensors to collect from various participating buildings in the city. The gap was everything that comes after the data: **what should a non-technical user actually do when a threshold is crossed?** My contribution is to design the Decision Support System (DSS) layer that bridges sensor readings and real-world action.

This is a **work in progress**, active across an internship and concurrently feeding a master's thesis.

## The Problem

A dashboard alone does not produce behavior change.

When CO₂ spikes in a classroom, a teacher needs to know: *open the window, for how long, and what to tell the children*. When PM2.5 is elevated across three buildings simultaneously, a facility manager needs prioritization logic, not three identical alerts. When a maintenance action is taken, the documentation trail required by Italian occupational health law (D.Lgs. 81/2008) must be low-friction enough that it actually gets done.

The existing dashboard surfaced data well. It did not close the loop to action.

I joined as a **UX and service design researcher** within Deda Next's innovation team, working alongside colleagues in product strategy, development, and project management. My scope:

- Evaluate the existing dashboard against usability and regulatory standards

## How I Framed the Problem (The Messy Part)
 
The first few weeks were less about designing and more about figuring out what kind of problem this actually was. The brief was open-ended: **make it useful for non-technical users.**
 
That framing hid a lot. "Non-technical users" turned out to be three very different people with almost nothing in common: **a teacher** who cares about their class and has maybe 90 seconds to act before a lesson starts; **a facility manager** juggling maintenance across five buildings simultaneously; and **a civil worker** who notices something feels off but has no formal channel to report it. Building one system for all three seemed increasingly wrong the more I mapped out their day-to-day contexts.
 
The early temptation was to reach for existing IAQ dashboard precedents like SAMHE in the UK and adapt them. These were useful reference points, but they all shared a common assumption: *that the person reading the dashboard has some baseline literacy about what PM2.5 or TVOC means and what to do about it*. That assumption did not hold here.

The clearer framing came from a different question: *at what moment does a user need to act, and what do they need to know to act confidently?* That reframe shifted the design problem from "how do we display data" to "how do we produce a decision."

I had to narrow it: is the gap about <em>awareness</em> (they don't see the alert), <em>interpretation</em> (they see it but don't understand it), or <em>protocol</em> (they understand it but have no guidance on what action to take)? ## Key Design Decisions

<section class="grid grid-cols-2 grid-rows-2 gap-6 mt-6">
  <article class="border col-span-2 rounded-lg p-6 shadow-sm bg-white">
    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold mb-3">Role Separation is Non-Negotiable</h4>
    <p class="mb-3 text-sm">The most consequential structural decision was splitting the interface into two distinct views:</p>
    <ul class="list-disc pl-5 mb-3 text-sm">
      <li><strong>Basic View</strong> — for teachers and office workers: minimal parameters, plain-language action guidance, single-location context</li>
      <li><strong>Advanced View</strong> — for facility staff: multi-location monitoring, severity-ranked alerts, compliance documentation flows</li>
    </ul>
    <p class="text-sm">Conflating these user groups produces cognitive overload for occupants and information loss for technical staff. The split is enforced at the information architecture level, not just as a toggle.</p>
  </article>

  <article class="border col-span-1 rounded-lg p-6 shadow-sm bg-white">
    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold mb-3">The Action Protocol Layer</h4>
    <p class="mb-3 text-sm">Drawing from the Boston Public Schools IAQ Response Action Plan precedent, I designed an explicit action protocol layer that sits between the sensor reading and the user. For each parameter threshold breach, the system surfaces:</p>
    <ol class="list-decimal pl-5 space-y-2 text-sm">
      <li>A plain-language severity label</li>
      <li>A context-specific action prompt (e.g., "Open two windows — cross-ventilation recommended")</li>
      <li>A one-tap logging action for compliance documentation</li>
      <li>A recovery status indicator to close the feedback loop</li>
    </ol>
  </article>

  <article class="border col-span-1 rounded-lg p-6 shadow-sm bg-white">
    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold mb-3">Wall-Mounted Tablet Display</h4>
    <p class="mb-3 text-sm">A secondary surface — a tablet mounted in classrooms and office spaces — needed to communicate ambient air quality status <em>passively</em>, without requiring active engagement. I designed five contextual display states:</p>
    <ul class="list-disc pl-5 space-y-2 text-sm">
      <li><strong>Ambient / Passive</strong> — ambient quality visible at a glance, no action required</li>
      <li><strong>Alert</strong> — threshold exceeded, action prompt displayed prominently</li>
      <li><strong>Action Taken / Recovery Tracking</strong> — confirms action logged, tracks parameter recovery</li>
      <li><strong>Reporting</strong> — allows occupants to submit a qualitative concern (H4)</li>
      <li><strong>Educational Context</strong> — surfaces contextual health information when parameters are near threshold (H5)</li>
    </ul>
  </article>
</section>

## Heuristic Evaluation

Before designing V2, I conducted a full **Nielsen Heuristic Evaluation** of the existing dashboard prototype, producing 30 documented issues:

- 2 Catastrophic
- 14 Major
- 11 Minor
- 3 Cosmetic

<ProjectImageAsset src="ediaqi-decision-support-system/heuristics-evaluation.jpg" alt="Description" />

A recurring critical finding: the dashboard applies **Classe II thresholds** uniformly across all building types, which may be legally inappropriate for school environments under Italian occupational health standards. This is flagged as a **design-resistant issue** requiring legal/institutional resolution, not a UI fix.

## What I'm Learning

The clearest thing this project has taught me is the difference between a design problem and a problem that design gets handed.
 
The sensor data is real, the thresholds are real, the regulatory requirements are real, but what I was asked to design was the layer that makes all of it usable by people who didn't ask for it and won't have time to learn it. That's a different kind of design challenge than building a product from a blank brief. You're working downstream of decisions you didn't make, and the constraints are often invisible until you run into them.
 
Working in Italian, within Italian law, for Italian public sector users, as someone who came to this project from outside that context — that gap has been as much a design constraint as any technical one. It has also, I think, made the work more honest about what it can and cannot do.
