## 01 // CONTEXT

You move into a new apartment. There's a courtyard downstairs, a couple of benches, a patch of grass. On paper it's a "common area." In practice, nobody uses it. The kids from the subsidized units play on one side. The families who bought at market price stay on the other. There is an unspoken tension between the two.

That gap, between a space that *exists* and a space that *belongs to people*, is what this project is about.

Mesa Te Club is a participatory design framework that turns awkward shared spaces in Chilean social housing into places people actually want to be in. By sitting them down with tea, snacks, and a set of playful tools and letting them design the spaces themselves, alongside developers, municipalities, and the housing ministry.

> _I led workshop design, research synthesis, and co-design tool creation across the full DS19 project timeline._

## 02 // PROBLEM

Chile has a housing program called DS19. On the surface, it's progressive, that is, private developers must reserve a percentage of units for vulnerable families, mixed in with market-rate buyers. The idea is reducing the socio-economic segregation that happens between people living in the same building.

## 03 // APPROACH

### 1. Mapping the ecosystem

Before designing anything, I needed to understand the forces at play. DS19 isn't just residents and architects, it's a system of different actors with different interests and unequal power dynamics.

### 2. A participation ladder, disguised as tea time

The framework has six activities, each designed for a different moment and a different kind of input. Together, they form a ladder from early idea crowdsourcing to post-occupancy learning.

### 3. Build-A-Space Lab & Playing Sims

Architects, planners, municipal social workers, and developers sit together and work through a **Common Area Canvas**. Residents receive a blank plan of their future building and a set of **pre-priced modular pieces** (playground, benches, gardens, courts) to negotiate layout priorities within budget constraints.

Through email, website, and WhatsApp, residents see the final chosen common areas. Months after delivery, residents and developers sit in the built common areas and play a custom version of "Never Have I Ever" to capture post-occupancy insights.

## 04 // IMPACT

- **Deployable Participatory Framework:** Demonstrated that structured co-design fits developer budgets and DS19 legal milestones while giving residents real spatial decision power.
- **Ritual-Based Co-Design:** Transformed traditional top-down meetings into approachable, tea-time design studio sessions using tangible modular tools.
