What’s Working Well ￼

Your UX research depth is genuinely strong. The EDIAQI case study stands out — falsifiable hypotheses, a clear “messy part” section where you show how your thinking evolved, and a meaningful reframe from “how do we display data” to “how do we produce a decision.” That kind of intellectual honesty is uncommon in junior/mid portfolios and hiring managers notice it.

Your writing is a differentiator. Each case study reads like a story with real stakes, not a sanitized process walkthrough. The Mesa Te Club opener — “There’s a courtyard downstairs… On paper it’s a ‘common area.’ In practice, nobody uses it” — immediately hooks. SuperEgo opens with a relatable moment that grounds a conceptual project. Most portfolio case studies are forgettable; yours aren’t.

Problem framing before solution. All three projects spend real time defining what the problem actually is before jumping to screens. EDIAQI maps three distinct user types and argues why a single dashboard fails them all. Mesa Te Club identifies that a participation ritual already existed and just needed design power. This signals design maturity.

Range of design methods. UX research (EDIAQI), behavioral design and dual-process theory (SuperEgo), participatory co-design and workshop facilitation (Mesa Te Club) — collectively, these show you can think across methodologies, not just push pixels.

Personality comes through. The sketching, calisthenics, hiking, and Substack essays make you feel like a real person. The Don Norman quote, the “Designed & Built with dual personality” footer — small touches that make the portfolio memorable.

Where It Falls Short for Product/UI/UX Designer Roles ￼

1. Visual and UI design is nearly invisible — this is the single biggest gap.

For a UI Designer role (like the Siemens Healthineers one you’re looking at), the portfolio needs to show visual craft: typography decisions, color systems, spacing rationale, component design, polished high-fidelity screens. Right now, your case studies describe wireframes and prototypes but the visual artifacts aren’t front and center. EDIAQI shows wireframes in the thumbnail; SuperEgo mentions a hi-fi prototype but the images are described rather than hero’d. A UI hiring manager will skim for 30 seconds looking for visual proof — they won’t find it quickly enough here.

2. No shipped or production work.

All three projects are academic — thesis work, internship, coursework. That’s fine for entry-level, but Product Designer roles increasingly want evidence you’ve dealt with real constraints: engineering handoff, stakeholder compromise, iterating on something live with real users. If you’ve done any freelance, side-project, or internship work that shipped, even small, it should be here.

3. Outcomes are vague.

EDIAQI says “30 usability issues identified” — good start, but what happened next? Were any resolved? Did behavior change? SuperEgo is a concept with no validation data. Mesa Te Club says “improved community engagement” — how? By what measure? Hiring managers want to see that your design decisions led to measurable change, even if the numbers are small.

4. The dual positioning dilutes your candidacy.

“Product Designer × Software Engineer” creates ambiguity. A design hiring manager wonders: is this person a designer who codes, or an engineer who also designs? Your LinkedIn says “UX Designer & Front-End Engineer” — yet another variation. For design roles, lead with design. Engineering skills are a bonus, not a co-headline. Pick one identity for the header and let the other emerge naturally in case studies.

5. Figma proficiency is claimed but not shown.

Every project tags Figma, but there are no embedded prototypes, no walkthrough videos, no design system screenshots, no component breakdowns. For a UI Designer role, showing how you work in Figma (systems thinking, auto-layout, variants, prototyping) is table stakes. Consider embedding interactive prototypes or at minimum annotated screen walkthroughs.

6. The Portfolio PDF link appears broken.

‎`/portfolio/file.pdf` — if that’s where your visual/UI work lives, it’s both buried and possibly inaccessible. That’s a credibility hit, especially when you’re positioning as someone who builds things.

7. No interaction or motion design.

Micro-interactions, transitions, responsive behavior, animation rationale — none of this appears anywhere. UI Designer roles increasingly expect this.

8. The copyright says 2025.

Small detail, but it signals the portfolio might not be current. Update it.

What I’d Prioritize Changing ￼

1. Add a dedicated “UI/Visual Design” section or project that shows pure craft — a design system contribution, a visual redesign, even a speculative one with clear rationale for every visual decision. This is non-negotiable for UI Designer roles.

2. Pick “Product Designer” or “UX Designer” as your primary identity. Drop the “× Software Engineer” from the headline. Mention your engineering background in your About section and let it surface naturally as a superpower, not a competing identity.

3. Embed interactive prototypes. Even Figma embeds or Loom walkthroughs — anything that lets a reviewer see you designing, not just writing about designing.

4. Fix the Portfolio PDF link and consider whether a PDF is even the right format. If it contains your best visual work, that work should be on this site directly.

5. Add outcomes wherever possible. Even qualitative ones: “After testing with 5 facility managers, 4 independently identified the action protocol as the feature they’d use daily.” Something concrete.

6. Align your LinkedIn headline with your portfolio positioning. Right now they tell slightly different stories.
